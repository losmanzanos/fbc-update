Is It Burnout or Depression? How to Tell the Difference

December 15, 2026  ·  By Becca Moravec, LPC, LMFT, AAMFT Approved Supervisor · 2 min read

“I think I’m just burned out.”

I hear that sentence a lot, usually from people who are exhausted, short-tempered and running on caffeine. Sometimes it’s exactly right. Sometimes it’s a gentler word for depression, one that feels easier to say out loud.

The difference matters, because what helps is different.

What Burnout Is

The World Health Organization describes burnout as an occupational phenomenon, not a medical diagnosis. It comes from chronic workplace stress that hasn’t been managed, and it has three hallmarks:

  1. Exhaustion
  2. Growing mental distance from your job, often cynicism or dread
  3. Feeling less effective at work

The common thread is a specific source. The WHO definition is about work, but most clinicians see the same pattern from caregiving, including parenting.

What Depression Is

Depression is broader. It doesn’t stay in one area of your life. The core signs are a persistent low mood or a loss of interest and pleasure in things you used to enjoy, lasting at least two weeks, along with changes in sleep, appetite, energy, concentration, or how you see yourself.

A Few Ways to Tell Them Apart

Does a vacation help? With burnout, a real break usually helps, at least for a while. With depression, you take the week off and feel just as flat at the beach.

Is it one area or everything? Burnout tends to stay in its lane. You dread Monday but still enjoy your Saturday hike. Depression bleeds into the hike, the friends, the shows you used to love.

How do you talk about yourself? Burnout sounds like “this job is impossible.” Depression more often sounds like “I’m the problem,” with guilt and worthlessness that don’t match the facts.

None of these are perfect tests. Burnout that goes on long enough can turn into depression, and plenty of people have both.

Why It Matters Which One

If it’s burnout, the fix has to include the source. Rest helps, but if you go back to the same 60-hour weeks with nothing changed, you’ll be back here by spring. Real solutions might mean a hard conversation with your manager, a different split of the load at home, or a bigger decision about the job.

If it’s depression, rest and a lighter schedule often aren’t enough on their own. Depression responds well to treatment, usually therapy and sometimes medication, but it rarely clears up because you took a long weekend.

Four Questions for This Week

  • If work disappeared tomorrow, would I feel better within a month?
  • When did I last really enjoy something?
  • Am I sleeping too much, too little, or about right?
  • Is my inner voice mostly tired, or mostly cruel?

Write down your answers. If you’re still unsure, bring them to a doctor or therapist. That’s exactly the information that helps us sort it out.

Getting Support

At Full Bloom we see a lot of high-functioning people who’ve been pushing through for years. Sometimes we’re helping them recover from burnout and renegotiate how they work. Sometimes we’re treating depression that’s been hiding under the word “busy.” Often it’s some of each.

If you’ve had thoughts that you’d be better off gone, please don’t wait. Call or text 988, any time. Otherwise, a free consultation is a good first step. It’s fifteen minutes, and you don’t need to know which one it is before you call.
