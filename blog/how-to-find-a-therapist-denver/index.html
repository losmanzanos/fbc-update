How to Find the Right Therapist in Denver (Without the Overwhelm)

June 15, 2026  ·  By Becca Moravec, LPC, LMFT · 7 min read

The moment you decide to try therapy is often the clearest moment in the whole process. Then comes the part that can feel like its own project: actually finding someone. Directories list thousands of names. The profiles all start to sound the same. There are questions about insurance and fit and waitlists, plus the low-grade dread of explaining yourself to a stranger before you even know they’re the right person.

This guide is meant to cut through that. We’ll walk through how to actually find a therapist in Denver, what to look for, what to ask, and how to know when you’ve found someone worth trying.

“The hardest part of finding a therapist usually isn’t the search. It’s the moment you decide you’re worth the effort of looking. Once you’ve cleared that bar, the rest is just logistics.”

Becca Moravec, LPC, LMFT, Founder of Full Bloom Counseling

Step One: Know (Roughly) What You’re Looking For

You don’t need a precise diagnosis or a clearly articulated problem to start therapy. But having some sense of what you’re hoping to address helps narrow the search meaningfully.

Are you dealing with something specific, like anxiety, relationship conflict, a recent loss, trauma, or disordered eating? Or is it more diffuse, a sense of being stuck or a feeling that something is off and you want to understand yourself better? Both are valid places to start. The difference is that specific concerns often benefit from a therapist with particular training (EMDR for trauma, say), while more exploratory work depends less on any one specialty.

Think about the practical side too. Do you need telehealth or in-person? What days and times actually work? Do you have strong preferences about the therapist’s identity or perspective? None of this is shallow. It affects whether you’ll actually stick with it.

Step Two: Where to Search

The most useful directories for finding Denver therapists:

  • Psychology Today. The largest directory, filterable by specialty, insurance, and location. Profile quality varies widely.
  • Alma. Tends to list therapists who accept insurance or post transparent sliding-scale fees. Good for budget-conscious searchers.
  • Therapy Den. Smaller, but leans toward therapists who explicitly identify as affirming, HAES-informed, or social-justice-oriented.
  • Direct search. Googling “EMDR therapist Denver” or “ADHD therapy Denver” often surfaces practice websites that tell you far more about a therapist than a directory listing ever could.

For online therapy across Colorado, geography opens up a lot. You’re no longer limited to Denver proper.

Step Three: Read Profiles Like a Human, Not a Checklist

Therapist profiles are easy to skim past. Slow down. You’re not only looking for credentials and specialties. You’re reading for voice, for how someone talks about their work, for whether anything resonates.

A therapist who writes “I provide a safe, non-judgmental space” tells you almost nothing. A therapist who writes specifically about how they think about trauma, or relationships, or the kind of client they do their best work with, is actually giving you information. Learn to notice the difference.

At Full Bloom, we’ve tried to make our therapist profiles genuinely useful, with more than credentials and bullet points. We want you to get a real sense of how each person works and who they work best with before you ever reach out.

Step Four: Use Free Consultations

Most therapists in Denver offer a free 15-20 minute phone or video consultation. Use them. All of them.

A consultation isn’t only about whether the therapist can help you. It’s about whether you feel at ease with them as a person. The research on therapy outcomes is unambiguous here: the quality of the therapeutic relationship is one of the strongest predictors of whether therapy works at all. No credential or specialty makes up for a poor fit.

Good questions to ask during a consultation:

  • How do you typically work with someone dealing with [your concern]?
  • What does a typical first few sessions look like with you?
  • What’s your approach if we hit a wall or I’m not making progress?
  • What are your fees, and do you offer sliding scale?

Notice how they answer. Are they direct? Do they seem curious about you? Do they give you a sense of how they actually work, or do they stay vague and generic?

Step Five: Give It a Real Try

I’ll admit something as the founder of a practice: I would much rather you tell me on session four that we’re not the right fit than quietly disappear and decide therapy doesn’t work. A good therapist has enough of their own support to not be wounded by that conversation. If yours seems fragile about it, that itself is useful information.

The first session is often awkward. You’re telling your story to a stranger, figuring out how this person works, recalibrating expectations. It’s normal to leave the first session unsure. The fit question is better answered after three or four sessions.

That said, if something feels genuinely off, not just uncomfortable but wrong, trust it. Good therapists expect clients to be discerning about fit. Saying “I don’t think this is the right match” isn’t rude, and we don’t take it personally.

A Note on Insurance and Fees in Denver

Many excellent Denver therapists, Full Bloom included, are out-of-network providers. That’s worth understanding rather than ruling out on reflex. If you have out-of-network benefits, you may be reimbursed for a significant portion of the cost, and HSA or FSA funds can cover the rest. Ask any therapist you’re considering to walk you through what the cost will really look like for you. Our guide to insurance and superbills in Colorado breaks it down step by step.
