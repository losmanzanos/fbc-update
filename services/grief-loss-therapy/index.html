Grief and loss therapy, without a timeline

Grief shows up in your sleep, your concentration, your body and your sense of who you are. Therapy gives it somewhere to go. We see people for every kind of loss, in person in Denver and online across Colorado.

Every kind of loss

Grief is bigger than bereavement

When people hear the word grief, they think of a death. That is a large part of what we see, but grief turns up anywhere something important has been lost.

  1. Death and bereavement

    A partner, parent, child, friend or pet, including sudden, traumatic and suicide loss.

  2. Divorce and breakups

    Grieving the person and also the future you had pictured together.

  3. Pregnancy loss and infertility

    Miscarriage, stillbirth, termination and the paths that didn’t happen. These losses are often minimized, and they deserve to be taken seriously.

  4. Illness and diagnosis

    Missing the body and the life you had before, and adjusting to a future that looks different.

  5. Ambiguous loss

    Loss without a clear ending, like estrangement, a parent with dementia or an adult child you’ve lost touch with.

  6. Identity and life changes

    Retirement, a career change, an empty house, and the person you were before that chapter closed.

Over time

Grief tends to come and go

You have probably heard grief described as a set of stages you work through in order. Most grieving people find it messier than that. A model proposed by researchers Margaret Stroebe and Henk Schut in the late 1990s describes healthy grieving as a back and forth. Some hours you turn toward the loss. Other hours you turn toward the rest of your life, and that is grieving too.

Turning toward the loss
  • Crying in the car
  • Looking through old photos
  • Missing their voice
  • Anger at how it happened
  • Rereading old texts
Turning toward the rest of life
  • Going back to work
  • Learning the bills they used to pay
  • A real laugh with a friend
  • New routines
  • An afternoon you forgot to be sad
The first weeksYears on

Early on

Many people describe the first weeks as foggy. You might cope better than you expected, then come apart over something small in the grocery store. Sleep, appetite and memory are often off.

Months in

The people around you often go quiet right when the loss starts to feel real. The paperwork is done and the meals have stopped, and you still reach for your phone to call them.

Years later

Birthdays, anniversaries and milestones can bring it all back, sometimes without warning. Over time most people find the grief changes weight more than it disappears, and there is room beside it for the rest of life.

None of this is a schedule. Some people feel the worst of it a year in. Some never feel the fog at all.

When to reach out

Signs grief could use more support

Grief is a normal response to loss, and you don’t need a diagnosis to benefit from talking with someone. It can help to come in when:

  • You feel stuck in the same raw place many months later, or numb when you expected to feel something
  • The loss was sudden or violent, or the result of suicide, and the images won’t leave you alone
  • Your relationship with the person was complicated, and you are grieving that too
  • Several losses have piled up close together
  • You avoid anything that reminds you of them, or you can’t think about anything else
  • The people around you seem to expect you to be over it by now

If you are having thoughts of suicide or of not wanting to be here, please call or text 988, the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time.

What sessions are like

Room for the whole of it

We don’t rush grief toward resolution. Most of the time it needs to be witnessed more than fixed.

Some weeks you’ll talk about the person: who they were, how they died, the story you keep telling yourself about the last conversation. Some weeks are practical, like getting through a holiday, going back to work, or deciding what to do with their things. There’s room for the anger, the numbness and the waves that hit out of nowhere.

Grief can also get complicated. The loss may carry trauma, the relationship may have been painful, or the loss may reopen older wounds. When that happens we can slow down and bring in trauma-informed work, and we pay attention to depression or anxiety that sometimes moves in alongside grief.

Grief counseling in Denver and across Colorado

Jillian Corpora sees clients in person at our Denver office near Glendale and online. Before becoming a therapist she worked in hospice care as a grief counselor, and she works with the death of someone close, the slow grief of watching someone you love get sick, and the losses people don’t always recognize. Kirsten Adorno works online with individuals and families anywhere in Colorado, including grief that is tangled up with family and relationships.

Sessions are 50 minutes and $150. We’re out of network with insurance and provide a superbill you can submit for possible reimbursement. Appointments run Monday to Friday from 8am to 8pm and weekends from 9am to 5pm, which helps when grief has already taken up most of your energy. If you’re not sure grief counseling is what you need, individual therapy is a fine place to start too.

Before you call

What people ask about grief counseling
