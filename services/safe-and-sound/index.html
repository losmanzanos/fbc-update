Safe and Sound Protocol for an overloaded nervous system

If you often feel overwhelmed, on edge or disconnected from yourself and others, your nervous system may be stuck in survival mode. The Safe and Sound Protocol (SSP) is a listening program designed to help it settle, so ordinary life stops registering as a threat.

What SSP is

Music filtered to sound like safety

The SSP was developed by Dr. Stephen Porges and is based on his Polyvagal Theory, which looks at how the nervous system responds to stress, safety and connection.

Many people, especially those who have lived through trauma or chronic stress, or who are neurodivergent, spend a lot of their time on high alert without realizing it. That can show up as constant tension, emotional reactivity, sensitivity to sound, or a hard time feeling close to people.

The SSP uses specially filtered music that emphasizes the frequency range of the human voice. According to Polyvagal Theory, that is the range our nervous system listens to when deciding whether we are safe. Over time the listening is meant to help your body shift out of survival mode, making calm, focus and connection easier to reach. It is often used alongside trauma therapy or as preparation for EMDR.

The program, laid out

Five hours of listening, spread over several weeks

The listening happens in short sittings through the MyUnyte app, in a calm and distraction-free space. Your therapist builds the plan with you and adjusts it as you go. Here is one way the program can be paced.

  1. IntakeGoals, history and your listening plan
  2. Hour 14 sittings of about 15 minutes
  3. Check-inHow your body is responding
  4. Hour 24 sittings
  5. Hour 34 sittings
  6. Check-inSlow down or keep going
  7. Hour 44 sittings
  8. Hour 54 sittings
  9. Check-inNotice what has shifted
  10. IntegrationWhat changed and what comes next
Session with your therapistCheck-in sessionAbout 15 minutes of listening

Before you listen

The intake session covers what brought you in, how your nervous system tends to react, and what you’re hoping changes. From there you get a listening plan that fits you.

While you listen

You listen at home, a little at a time, somewhere calm where you won’t be interrupted. Check-in sessions let us slow the pace if you feel stirred up, or keep going if it’s sitting well.

After the last hour

The final integration session looks at what has shifted and what’s next. Changes often show up over the days and weeks after listening, so we keep paying attention after the music ends.

Your plan may look different from this. Many people go slower, and that’s fine. Results vary from person to person.

Who it can help

Who tends to come to us for SSP

People most often try the protocol when their body seems to stay on high alert, or shut down, even when they know they’re safe.

  • Anxiety and depression, when worry and low mood won’t settle
  • Trauma and PTSD, when your body still reacts as if the danger hasn’t passed
  • Neurodivergence, including sensory overload with autism, ADHD and sound sensitivity
  • Chronic stress and burnout, when rest doesn’t feel restful anymore
  • Kids with sensory or developmental challenges, including sound sensitivity and attention
  • Relational and attachment wounds, when closeness feels risky even with people you trust

SSP often pairs well with anxiety therapy and with somatic work. It isn’t a stand-alone fix, and we use it as one part of therapy.

What people ask about SSP

SSP works with the autonomic nervous system, below the level of thinking things through. It doesn’t require you to talk about traumatic experiences, and it is often used to prepare for, or support, other trauma work like EMDR.
