HAES and Intuitive Eating therapy, minus the diet talk

Weight-inclusive therapy in Denver and online across Colorado for people who are tired of fighting their bodies. We focus on how you treat yourself, and we leave the scale out of it.

01The framework

Health without a number attached

Health at Every Size, or HAES, starts from a plain observation: weight is a poor stand-in for health, and chasing a smaller body has cost a lot of people their peace with food.

The HAES principles are stewarded by the Association for Size Diversity and Health. They separate health behaviors from body size, and they treat weight stigma as a real source of harm. They also take seriously what research and most people’s own history show: diets rarely produce lasting weight loss, and the cycle of losing and regaining tends to leave people more preoccupied with food than when they started.

In therapy, that means we never set weight-loss goals and we never weigh you. We pay attention to eating that answers to your body’s signals, movement you might enjoy, sleep, stress, and the way you talk to yourself. Some clients come in after twenty years on and off diets. Others just want food to take up less room in their head.

02Intuitive Eating

The ten principles, in plain words

Intuitive Eating was created by two dietitians, Evelyn Tribole and Elyse Resch, and first published as a book in 1995. Its ten principles are a way back to trusting your own hunger, fullness and satisfaction. They aren’t a new set of rules to pass or fail.

  1. Reject the Diet Mentality

    Letting go of the hope that the next plan will be the one. If diet after diet hasn’t worked, that tells you something about diets.

  2. Honor Your Hunger

    Eating enough, and regularly, so your body stops sounding the alarm. Hunger that gets ignored for long enough tends to come back as eating that feels out of control.

  3. Make Peace with Food

    Giving yourself real permission to eat. The foods you forbid are usually the ones you can’t stop thinking about.

  4. Challenge the Food Police

    Talking back to the voice that calls you good for skipping dessert and bad for having it.

  5. Discover the Satisfaction Factor

    Eating food you actually like, somewhere you can enjoy it. A satisfying meal makes “enough” much easier to recognize.

  6. Feel Your Fullness

    Noticing the point where you’re comfortably full. That gets easier once you trust more food will be there later.

  7. Cope with Your Emotions with Kindness

    Finding comfort that doesn’t all run through food, without shaming yourself for the times it does.

  8. Respect Your Body

    Treating the body you have right now with basic dignity: clothes that fit, rest, care. You don’t have to love it to respect it.

  9. Movement: Feel the Difference

    Moving because it feels good, with the calorie math left out. Walks count. So do rest days.

  10. Honor Your Health with Gentle Nutrition

    Choosing food that supports your health and still tastes good. It comes last on purpose, once the rules have loosened their grip.

We work through these at your pace and in whatever order makes sense for you. Plenty of people spend months on the first three, and that’s fine.

03Is this for you

People who are worn out by diet culture

You don’t need a diagnosis or a dramatic story. If any of these sound familiar, this work is probably a good fit.

  • You’ve started over on Monday more times than you can count
  • Certain foods feel off limits, and then they’re all you can think about
  • You exercise to earn food, or to make up for it afterward
  • You skip the pool, the photos, or the party because of how you think you’ll look
  • Your body changed with pregnancy, illness, medication, or age, and you’re struggling with it
  • You’re in eating disorder recovery and want a weight-neutral therapist
  • You’re a parent who doesn’t want to pass food worries on to your kids
  • You want health support that doesn’t open with your weight

Questions about HAES therapy
