A wide spectrum

What disordered eating can look like

Most of it never shows up on a scale or in a diagnosis. If food or your body is causing you distress, that’s reason enough to get help.

  1. Restriction

    Limiting what or how much you eat out of fear, guilt or a need for control. It’s often invisible to the people around you.

  2. Binge eating

    Episodes of eating that feel out of control, frequently followed by shame, secrecy, or trying to make up for it.

  3. Food preoccupation

    So much mental energy spent on food, calories, weight or your body that there’s little room for anything else.

  4. Body image distress

    Persistent shame or vigilance about your body’s size or shape, and the plans you cancel because of it.

  5. Chronic dieting

    A long history of starting and stopping diets, and the sense of failure that cycles along with them.

  6. Compensating

    Exercise as punishment, laxatives, skipping meals after eating: anything aimed at undoing food.

Disordered eating usually has less to do with food than it looks. Food and your body come to stand for other things: control, safety, comfort, sometimes punishment. It is often tangled up with anxiety, perfectionism, trauma, and the wish for one part of life to feel manageable. We work with all of that, along with the behaviors you can see.

Inner rules

Food rules you might recognize

Nobody hands these to you on a list. They pile up over years, from diets and family dinners and comments you heard once at eleven years old. See how many sound familiar.

  • No eating after seven.
  • If I eat one, I’ll eat the whole box.
  • I have to earn breakfast.
  • Start over on Monday.
  • Eat less than everyone else at the table.
  • Never keep chips in the house.
  • I already blew it today, so it doesn’t matter.
  • Fruit is fine. Bread is not.
  • Order the salad when we go out.
  • The scale decides what kind of day it is.
  • Dessert only if I skipped lunch.
  • Don’t be the first one back for seconds.
  • Run off whatever I ate last night.
  • Hungry means I’m doing it right.
  • If I can’t log it, I shouldn’t eat it.
  • I’ll relax about food once I lose ten pounds.
  • Carbs are for days I worked out.
First

Find where it came from

Most rules have a history: a diet that worked for a month, a parent’s comment, a season when control felt necessary. Naming the origin takes away some of its authority.

Then

Ask what it’s protecting

Rules usually have a job. They keep anxiety down, or hold off the feeling of being out of control. We take that job seriously before asking the rule to retire.

Over time

Loosen it, at your pace

With support, you test a rule in small ways and see what actually happens. Some fade quickly. Others take months, and that’s a normal part of recovery.

Level of care

Where outpatient therapy fits

Eating disorders affect people of every body size, age and gender. You don’t have to look sick to be struggling.

What we offer is outpatient therapy: one 50-minute session a week, sometimes more often at the start. That’s the right level of care for many people, including many in recovery from an eating disorder, people working through binge eating, and people coming off decades of dieting. We take a Health at Every Size approach, so weight loss is never a treatment goal and progress isn’t measured by your body.

When weekly therapy isn’t enough

Some eating disorders need medical monitoring, and outpatient therapy alone isn’t safe enough. Higher levels of care exist for that, including intensive outpatient, partial hospitalization and residential programs with medical and nutrition staff.

Please see a doctor soon if you’re fainting or dizzy, purging often, losing weight quickly, noticing a racing or irregular heartbeat, or eating very little for days at a time. You can still reach out to us. We’ll help you work out the right level of care, and refer you if it isn’t us.

If you’re in crisis or thinking about hurting yourself, call or text 988 to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline, any time of day.
