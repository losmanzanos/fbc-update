Trauma therapy so the past can feel like the past

Trauma doesn’t stay in the past on its own. It lives in your body, your reactions and the stories you tell yourself about what’s safe. Healing it rarely means reliving everything. We see clients in Denver, in Boulder and online across Colorado.

Where it shows up

How trauma can show up years later

You don’t need a PTSD diagnosis, or a story that sounds dramatic from the outside, for trauma therapy to help. Trauma is less about how bad an event looks and more about how your system responded to it. Most people notice it in one or more of these places.

In the body

Being on alert all the time, sleep that never feels deep, tension you’ve stopped noticing, or going foggy and far away when stress hits. Your body may react before your mind has caught up.

In relationships

Trouble trusting, bracing for people to leave, keeping everyone at arm’s length, or saying yes to keep the peace. Closeness itself can start to feel risky.

In what you believe

“It was my fault.” “I’m not safe.” “I can’t trust myself.” Beliefs formed in hard moments tend to stay long after the moment is over.

Trauma doesn’t only live in memory. It shows up as a jaw that won’t unclench, a startle that’s too big, or a strange flatness when you should feel something.Kelli Ruhl, LPC, NCC, LAC · Read her bio →
Who we see

The kinds of trauma we work with

If past experiences are still shaping how you feel in your body, how you relate to people or how safe the world seems, that is enough of a reason.

  1. PTSD and complex PTSD

    A single event, or years of difficult experiences that added up. We work with both.

  2. Childhood and developmental trauma

    Wounds formed early, often before you had words for them, that shaped your sense of self and safety.

  3. Relational and attachment trauma

    Early bonds that were frightening or unreliable, and still affect how you connect and trust.

  4. Sexual assault and abuse

    Survivor-centered care that moves at your pace, with your consent at every step.

  5. Medical trauma and accidents

    A hospital stay, a birth, a crash, or anything that left your nervous system stuck on alert.

  6. Religious trauma and traumatic grief

    Fear and shame tied to a faith community, and losses that were sudden, violent or unexpected.

Our methods

Approaches that work with the body as well as the story

Talking about trauma isn’t always the right first step. Sometimes the body needs to feel steadier before the memory can be touched. Our therapists combine these approaches based on what you need.

EMDR

Helps the brain reprocess memories that still feel present, often without describing them in detail.

You don’t have to retell every detail of what happened for things to change.

Pacing

Steady ground first, then the harder work

Most trauma specialists recommend working in phases, especially with complex trauma. In practice the phases overlap, and we move back and forth as needed.

Getting steady

We build safety in the room and skills for the rest of the week: grounding, sleep, what to do when you get flooded. For some people this part alone changes a lot.

Working with what happened

When you’re ready, we work with the memories and beliefs that still carry a charge, using EMDR, parts work or somatic approaches. You set the pace and can pause at any time.

Living after it

As the past quiets down, the focus shifts to the present: relationships, rest, trusting yourself, and the parts of life trauma kept you from.

Good to know

Questions about trauma therapy

Trauma therapy focuses on how difficult experiences are stored in the brain and body. Methods like EMDR and somatic therapy work directly with that, alongside the thinking patterns and beliefs that formed around what happened.

No. EMDR and somatic approaches in particular don’t require you to describe events in detail to work. We move at a pace your nervous system can tolerate, and you decide what you share.
